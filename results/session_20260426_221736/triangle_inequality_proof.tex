\begin{proof}
\textbf{1. Norm as inner product.}  
By definition of the norm induced by an inner product, for any vector \(x\) we have  
\[
\|x\|^{2}= \langle x, x\rangle .
\]

\textbf{2. Expansion of \(\|x+y\|^{2}\).}  
Using bilinearity and symmetry of the inner product,
\begin{align*}
\langle x+y, x+y\rangle &= \langle x, x\rangle + \langle x, y\rangle + \langle y, x\rangle + \langle y, y\rangle \\
&= \langle x, x\rangle + 2\langle x, y\rangle + \langle y, y\rangle .
\end{align*}
Hence
\[
\|x+y\|^{2}= \|x\|^{2} + 2\langle x, y\rangle + \|y\|^{2}.
\]

\textbf{3. Cauchy–Schwarz bound.}  
The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states \(|\langle x, y\rangle| \le \|x\|\,\|y\|\).  
Since \(\langle x, y\rangle \le |\langle x, y\rangle|\), we obtain
\[
\langle x, y\rangle \le \|x\|\,\|y\| \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
2\langle x, y\rangle \le 2\|x\|\,\|y\|.
\]

\textbf{4. Bounding the squared norm.}  
Substituting the estimate from step 3 into the expression of step 2 gives
\begin{align*}
\|x+y\|^{2}
&= \|x\|^{2} + 2\langle x, y\rangle + \|y\|^{2} \\
&\le \|x\|^{2} + 2\|x\|\,\|y\| + \|y\|^{2} \\
&= (\|x\|+\|y\|)^{2}.
\end{align*}

\textbf{5. Taking square roots.}  
Both sides of the inequality in step 4 are non‑negative, so the square‑root function is monotone. Hence
\[
\|x+y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|.
\]

\textbf{6. Equality condition.}  
Equality in step 4 occurs exactly when equality holds in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality used in step 3, i.e. when
\[
|\langle x, y\rangle| = \|x\|\,\|y\|.
\]
The equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz states that this happens iff the vectors are linearly dependent: there exists a scalar \(\lambda\) such that \(y = \lambda x\). Moreover, for the inequality in step 3 to be an equality we need \(\langle x, y\rangle = \|x\|\,\|y\|\), which forces \(\lambda \ge 0\). Thus equality in the triangle inequality holds precisely when \(y = \lambda x\) with \(\lambda \ge 0\) (the vectors point in the same direction).

Therefore the triangle inequality \(\|x+y\| \le \|x\|+\|y\|\) holds for all vectors \(x, y\) in an inner‑product space, with equality exactly when \(x\) and \(y\) are positively proportional. \(\square\)
\end{proof}
